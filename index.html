Today's route, in plain words

1. Everyone starts at the van, parked on the road next to D42.
2. Carry at most 60 clothes at a time. Do the trips in the order written below.
3. Inside each building, visit the floors in the order written below. You never climb the same stairs twice.
4. If a room is locked, knock, wait a minute, and move on. Mark it "Not in" on your phone.
5. After each trip, check your phone (Walker mode). It says "go back to the van" or "go straight to your next trip".

      How the model works, briefly

      1. Campus. Every block (D19–D43, MSH 1–19) was traced from IIMA's official campus map. The drawing was turned into a flat plan, scaled so the new campus covers its 39 acres. Footpaths link each block to its neighbours, and Dijkstra gives the shortest walk between any two blocks.
      2. Cost of a trip. No fixed charge per trip. A trip costs its minutes: walking, plus stairs, plus knocking and counting at each room, plus 1 minute to unload at the van. A day costs the team's time: everyone is paid until the last person is back, with 50% extra for overtime.
      3. Routes. Three methods from class (nearest-neighbour, sweep, Clarke–Wright) and our method. Our method uses a known idea from research, "route first, cut second" (Beasley 1983; Prins 2004): make one long tour, then cut it into armloads with a shortest-path model. We seed it with Clarke–Wright and improve it by moving rooms between trips. Every method finishes with 2-opt, and trips are shared out with the least-utilised-bin rule.
      4. No-shows (our own addition). On the day, after each trip, the app decides whether to walk back to the van or carry on with what you have. It works out the expected minutes of each choice. The chance a student is out starts from the case probabilities and is updated by Bayes' rule after every knock.
      5. Choosing. Each method is simulated on the same random days for each kind of day. The case probabilities weight the results, and the lowest expected cost wins.
